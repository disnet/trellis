I think the deepest problem with the chatbot frame isn't the wall of text, it's that the _transcript_ is the primary artifact. A transcript is history. Thinking needs state. Everything else follows from choosing the wrong primary object.

So the first move: **the agent's output is a diff to a persistent structure, not a message appended to a log.** When the agent does something, the result should be "three nodes changed, one new link proposed, one contradiction flagged," not four paragraphs you have to read to find out what it thinks. The chat can still exist, but as compost: an ephemeral scratch space where messy exploration happens and gets distilled into the graph. What persists is the graph.

Second, invert the context problem. In chat, the human labors to keep the right things in the agent's view. In a graph-native system, **selection is context.** You point at a region (a node, a cluster, a view) and that's what the agent is working within. The graph itself is the shared memory. This is what makes non-linear thinking cheap: you jump, the agent follows, no re-explaining. Long-running agent tasks fit naturally too, because they post results back to the graph rather than blocking a turn-taking loop.

Third, on the unit. Obsidian's page is a document; documents are for reading, not manipulating. I'd go smaller and more typed: the atom is a claim, question, concept, or example, with links that mean something (supports, contradicts, depends on, instance of, supersedes). Typed links are what let an agent do real work rather than summarize. It can find that two nodes written six months apart say opposite things, notice a claim that every downstream idea depends on is still marked "tentative," or spot that you've reinvented a concept under a new name. That's the librarian role, and it's more valuable than the oracle role.

Fourth, and this is the constraint that keeps it from becoming slop: **the agent proposes, the human ratifies, and ratification is where the thinking happens.** A PKB is only valuable if the reified concepts are yours. If the agent writes the graph and you skim it, you've built a very sophisticated document you don't understand. So the interface should make acceptance a deliberate act, something like tracked changes or pull requests for thought, with a staging area where agent proposals wait. The friction there isn't a bug. And nodes should carry provenance: who wrote this, when, based on what, and what its status is (tentative, believed, contested, retired). Reified concepts should age visibly.

Fifth, multiple views over the same graph, generated on demand. Outline, canvas, argument map, dependency tree, timeline. "Show me everything downstream of this assumption" produces a view, not an essay. The agent's job is to render the structure so you can see it, and your job is to look and decide.

Put together, a session might look like: you drop a messy paragraph into scratch. The agent comes back with "this seems to contain two claims and one question; the second claim conflicts with node X from March; want to reify?" You accept one, edit one, reject one. Later you open the canvas, drag three clusters together, and ask "what's the strongest objection to this whole region?" It answers with a proposed node linked as _contradicts_, and you decide whether it earns a place.

The hard open problems, as I see them: what granularity of chunk is right (too small and it's a database, too big and it's Obsidian again), how to keep agent-generated structure from crowding out your own, and whether typed links survive contact with real messy thought or collapse back into "related." My guess is the answer to the last one is that the agent maintains the types and you mostly just write, which is the division of labor the whole design depends on.
